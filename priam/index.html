PRIAM Precision Radio Instrument
for Antenna Measurements

A flying calibration source. PRIAM measures the magnitude and phase response of an antenna-under-test in the far field, from a hovering hexacopter with centimetre-level RTK positioning — built for 21 cm cosmology and low-frequency radio instruments.

The problem

Antennas are rarely calibrated where they are used.

Low-frequency radio experiments — especially those chasing the redshifted 21 cm line — live and die by how well they know their beams and their spectral response. A dish or a dipole is normally characterised with a fixed boresight tower, a far-field range, or a celestial source. All three are expensive, geometry-limited, or far too dim.

PRIAM inverts the arrangement. Instead of bringing the antenna to a test range, it flies a known transmitter to the antenna. A hexacopter carries a software-defined radio and a switchable dual-polarization crossed dipole, hovers at a sequence of precisely known positions in the far field, and records the received signal on the ground.

Because the source position is known to the centimetre via RTK GPS — and because the payload records phase against a common frequency reference — the measurement yields a full complex response of the antenna-under-test, in amplitude and phase, across the sky.

  • Far-field sampling without a tower or a turntable
  • Complex (magnitude + phase) beam mapping
  • Dual-linear and circular polarization calibration
  • Repeatable flight paths from a generated trajectory

Testbed

Flamewheel F450

A scaled-down drone for experimentation and testing

The small airframe carries the risk. It was used to validate the custom ROS nodes, prove out RTK performance in the air, and trial new versions of the flight stack before they ever touched the full payload. It also hosted feature evaluations such as RPM-based gyro filtering and system-model verification.

Science payload

Everything needed to make a trustworthy measurement, in the air.

The payload is a self-contained radio instrument: its own computer, its own power, its own frequency reference, and an antenna that can be reconfigured in flight.

01

Payload computer

Raspberry Pi 4B with an uninterruptible power supply and its own battery, isolated from the flight battery. It schedules the SDR, commands the RF switch, and streams captures to storage.

02

Software-defined radio

Ettus B205-mini covering 70 MHz to 6 GHz. Wide instantaneous bandwidth makes broadband antenna characterisation practical in a single pass.

03

Frequency reference

A high-accuracy reference disciplines the payload so that phase remains coherent across the flight — the difference between a beam map and a phase measurement.

04

Antenna system

Dual-polarization, half-wavelength, telescopic crossed dipoles tunable from 70 MHz to 1 GHz, fed through a custom dual-polarization balun.

05

RF switching

A custom switch network selects X, Y, or circular polarization, so one flight can deliver a full polarization set instead of one.

06

Geometry

RTK GPS fixes each measurement point to the centimetre, tying every sample to a known far-field direction and range.

Custom RF hardware

Boards designed for this experiment.

Nothing off the shelf does polarization switching at these wavelengths on a flying platform, so the RF chain was designed in-house. Sources in Microwave Office, Eagle, and Fusion 360 live in the RF Related folder.

RF switches

Two designs. A simple switch enables X- and Y-polarization calibration; a larger network of RF switches extends the same idea to X-, Y-, and circular-polarization calibration.

  • X / Y selection
  • X / Y / circular
  • Flight-rated layout

Dual-pol balun

A vertically oriented, dual-polarization balun that feeds the crossed dipoles. It is the mechanical and electrical interface between the payload's coax runs and the radiating elements.

  • Dual polarization
  • Vertical orientation
  • 70 MHz – 1 GHz
